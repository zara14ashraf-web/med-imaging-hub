How Are X-rays Produced?

Ever wondered what actually happens inside an X-ray tube before an image appears on the screen?

5 min read · Medical Imaging

First, What Exactly Is an X-ray?

An X-ray is a form of electromagnetic radiation. It has enough energy to pass through the human body, which is why it can be used to create medical images.

But the X-rays used in medical imaging do not simply come from nowhere. They are produced inside a special device called an X-ray tube.

So, How Does the X-ray Tube Produce Them?

The process mainly involves two important parts of the X-ray tube: the cathode and the anode.

  • The cathode contains a filament that releases electrons.
  • A high voltage accelerates these electrons toward the anode.
  • The electrons hit a target material, usually tungsten.
  • Their sudden interaction with the target produces X-rays.
Think of it like this:

Imagine throwing a fast-moving ball at a hard wall. When the ball suddenly stops or changes direction, energy is released. In an X-ray tube, fast-moving electrons hit the target and their energy is converted into X-ray photons.

What Happens to the Electrons?

When electrons strike the tungsten target, most of their energy is converted into heat. Only a small portion is converted into X-rays.

This is one reason why the X-ray tube needs to be designed to handle a large amount of heat.

Where Do the X-rays Go?

The X-rays produced at the target leave the tube through a small opening. They then travel toward the patient.

As the X-ray beam passes through the body, different tissues absorb different amounts of radiation. The remaining X-rays reach the detector and are used to create the image.

Key Takeaway

X-rays are produced inside an X-ray tube when high-speed electrons from the cathode strike a target at the anode. Their interaction produces X-ray photons, which then pass through the patient and help create the final image.
