How Does Ultrasound Create an Image?

See how sound waves travel through the body and return information that becomes an ultrasound image.

Image: Harrison Keely, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0.

When we look at an ultrasound image, it can seem almost like the machine is simply taking a picture of the inside of the body. But that is not actually what happens.

Ultrasound creates an image by sending high-frequency sound waves into the body and listening for the echoes that come back. The machine then uses information from those echoes to build an image.

So, in a simple way, ultrasound imaging can be thought of as:

Sound waves → Body tissues → Echoes → Computer processing → Image

1. It Starts With the Transducer

The handheld part of an ultrasound machine is called the transducer, or probe.

The transducer has special materials inside it called piezoelectric elements. These elements can convert electrical energy into mechanical vibrations and can also convert returning vibrations back into electrical signals.

This is what allows the same transducer to both send sound waves and receive echoes.

Image: Harrison Keely, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0.

2. The Probe Sends Sound Waves Into the Body

The ultrasound machine sends electrical signals to the transducer. The piezoelectric elements respond by vibrating and producing high-frequency sound waves.

These sound waves are above the range that humans can normally hear, which is why they are called ultrasound.

The sound waves travel from the transducer into the body.

3. Why Do Echoes Come Back?

As the sound waves travel through the body, they encounter boundaries between different tissues.

Different tissues have different acoustic properties. When the sound wave reaches a boundary where the tissue properties change, part of the sound energy can be reflected back toward the transducer.

This returning sound is called an echo.

Some tissues produce stronger echoes, while others produce weaker echoes. This difference is one of the reasons ultrasound images contain different shades of black, gray, and white.

Think of it like this: the ultrasound probe sends sound into the body, and the returning echoes carry information about the tissues the sound encountered.

4. The Echo Comes Back to the Probe

After sending a sound pulse, the transducer switches to receiving mode and listens for returning echoes.

The returning mechanical vibrations affect the piezoelectric elements inside the transducer. These vibrations are converted back into electrical signals.

The ultrasound system can then analyze those signals.

Send Sound pulse enters the body
Receive Echoes return to the probe
Process Machine analyzes the signals

5. Time Helps Determine Depth

One important piece of information is how long it takes for an echo to return.

If an echo returns quickly, it generally came from a structure closer to the transducer. If it takes longer to return, the reflecting structure is generally deeper.

The ultrasound system uses the travel time of the sound pulse and its returning echo to estimate the depth of structures.

This information helps the machine decide where to place the information in the image.

6. Echo Strength Helps Create Bright and Dark Areas

The machine also looks at the strength of the returning echo.

A stronger echo can appear brighter on the image, while a weaker echo can appear darker.

This creates different shades of gray and allows structures with different echo characteristics to appear differently on the screen.

For example, simple fluid-filled areas often produce very few internal echoes and therefore appear relatively dark. Highly reflective structures such as bone surfaces can appear very bright, often with a darker acoustic shadow behind them.

7. The Machine Turns Echo Information Into Pixels

At this point, the machine has a large amount of information:

  • When an echo returned
  • How strong the echo was
  • Where the sound pulse was sent

The system processes this information and assigns brightness values to different locations in the image.

These values are displayed as pixels, producing the familiar black, white, and gray ultrasound image.

8. And This Happens Again and Again

Ultrasound does not send just one sound wave and create one image. The system sends many pulses and receives many echoes.

The transducer scans across the region of interest, collecting echo information from different locations.

The machine processes these signals very quickly and continuously updates the display.

This is why ultrasound can provide real-time imaging. We can watch structures move while the examination is happening.

The Whole Process in One Simple Flow

Electrical signal → Transducer → Sound waves → Body tissues → Returning echoes → Electrical signals → Computer processing → Ultrasound image

Another simple way to remember it is:

1. Send The probe sends sound waves.
2. Receive The probe receives echoes.
3. Measure The system analyzes timing and echo strength.
4. Display The information becomes an image.

What Does the Gel Actually Do?

The gel placed on the skin is not just there to make the probe move smoothly.

Air is a major problem for ultrasound because a layer of air between the transducer and skin can greatly reduce the transmission of ultrasound into the body.

Ultrasound gel helps remove that air gap and provides better acoustic coupling between the transducer and the skin.

Simple idea: the gel helps the ultrasound waves move efficiently from the probe into the body and back toward the probe.

Why Do Different Tissues Look Different?

Different tissues interact with ultrasound in different ways.

Their physical properties affect how much sound is reflected, scattered, transmitted, or attenuated as the ultrasound wave travels through them.

Because the returning echoes are different, the ultrasound system displays different areas with different brightness levels.

This is why an ultrasound image is not simply a photograph of anatomy. It is a visual representation created from the interaction between sound waves and tissues.

One More Important Point: Ultrasound Does Not “See” Like a Camera

This is one of the easiest ways to understand ultrasound.

A camera records visible light reflected from objects. An ultrasound system works differently.

It sends sound pulses into the body, receives the returning echoes, measures their characteristics, and uses that information to construct the image.

So the image on the screen is essentially a map of ultrasound echo information.

What About Doppler?

The same basic ultrasound system can also be used for Doppler ultrasound.

Doppler techniques use changes in the frequency of returning ultrasound caused by moving blood cells to provide information about blood flow.

This is why Doppler can help assess things such as the direction and velocity of blood flow.

So while standard B-mode ultrasound mainly creates a grayscale anatomical image, Doppler adds information about motion and blood flow.

The Big Picture

Ultrasound image formation may seem complicated at first, but the basic idea is quite simple:

The transducer sends sound waves → tissues produce echoes → the transducer receives those echoes → the machine analyzes them → the information is displayed as an image.

Once this basic process is clear, many ultrasound concepts become easier to understand — including echogenicity, acoustic shadowing, posterior enhancement, B-mode imaging, and Doppler.
