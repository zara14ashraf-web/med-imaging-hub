Why Do Bones Look White While Lungs Look Dark on an X-ray?

The simple science behind the different shades we see on an X-ray image.

5 min read · Medical Imaging

It All Comes Down to How X-rays Pass Through the Body

When an X-ray beam passes through the body, it does not travel through every tissue in exactly the same way. Different tissues absorb different amounts of X-ray radiation.

This difference is what creates the shades we see on an X-ray. Some structures appear white, some appear grey, and others appear almost black.

Why Do Bones Appear White?

Bone is relatively dense and contains minerals such as calcium. Because of its density and composition, bone absorbs or attenuates a large amount of the X-ray beam.

This means fewer X-rays reach the detector behind the bone. The result is a bright or white appearance on the radiograph.

Think of it like a wall:

A thick wall blocks much of the light passing through it. Similarly, dense bone blocks more X-rays, so less radiation reaches the detector and the area appears white.

Why Do Lungs Appear Dark?

The lungs are different because they contain a large amount of air. Air allows most X-rays to pass through with relatively little attenuation.

More X-rays therefore reach the detector through the lungs. On a conventional radiograph, this produces a darker appearance.

What About Soft Tissues?

Soft tissues such as muscles, organs, and blood have attenuation characteristics between those of bone and air.

As a result, they usually appear in different shades of grey. Their visibility can also depend on the surrounding structures and the technique used to acquire the image.

So, What Creates the X-ray Contrast?

The key idea is attenuation. When different tissues attenuate the X-ray beam by different amounts, the detector receives different amounts of radiation from different parts of the body.

These differences are converted into the shades that form the radiographic image.

A simple way to remember it:

More attenuation → fewer X-rays reach the detector → whiter appearance.
Less attenuation → more X-rays reach the detector → darker appearance.

Key Takeaway

Bones appear white because they attenuate a large amount of the X-ray beam, while the air-filled lungs attenuate much less. The difference in X-ray transmission is what creates the contrast between white, grey, and dark areas on a radiograph.
