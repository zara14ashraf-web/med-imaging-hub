01 · AI & IMAGING

What does AI actually look at in a medical image?

We see anatomy. But what does a computer actually see?

↓
Start with the basics

When we look at an X-ray, MRI, or ultrasound image, we usually see things we have learned to recognize: bones, organs, blood vessels, lesions, or areas that look different from normal.

An AI model does not look at an image in the same human way. It processes the image as numerical data and looks for patterns within that data.

So what does AI “see”?
It sees numbers representing the image and uses patterns learned from previous examples to make a prediction.
How does that work?

During training, an AI model is shown many medical images together with their known labels or outcomes. For example, it may be shown ultrasound images labelled as benign or malignant.

The model gradually learns which visual patterns are useful for separating one group from another. It can then use those patterns when it sees a new image.

But here's the interesting part

If an AI model gives the correct answer, that does not automatically tell us which part of the image helped it reach that answer.

Maybe it focused on the lesion. Maybe it used a texture, shape, or intensity pattern. And sometimes, it may even use a pattern that we did not expect.

Techniques such as Grad-CAM can create a visual map showing which areas of an image contributed more strongly to a model's prediction. This can help researchers investigate whether the model is focusing on a medically meaningful region. It does not show exactly what the AI “thinks,” but it can provide useful evidence about where the model is looking.
My Thought

When I first started exploring AI in medical imaging, one question kept coming back to me: if a model gives the right answer, how do we know what it actually looked at?

So, when AI looks at a medical image… are we really sure we know what it sees?
02 · AI & IMAGING

Does AI understand an image — or recognize patterns?

A simple question that changes how we think about medical AI.

↓
Imagine this

Imagine showing an AI thousands of ultrasound images labelled as benign or malignant.

The model studies these examples during training. It does not receive a textbook definition of a tumour and then understand it like a student would.

Instead, it learns patterns in the examples that help it distinguish between the categories it has been trained to recognize.

What a human can use

• Anatomy

• Clinical knowledge

• Patient information

• Previous experience

VS

What an AI model uses

• Patterns in images

• Information learned from data

• Relationships between features

• Prediction

Does that mean AI understands?

This is where things become interesting.

An AI model can become extremely good at recognizing patterns associated with a particular outcome. But recognizing those patterns is not necessarily the same as understanding the medical meaning of an image.

For example, if certain features repeatedly appear in images labelled as malignant, the model may learn that those features are useful for making its prediction. But it does not have the same understanding of disease, anatomy, or patient history that a healthcare professional brings to the examination.

The important idea:
A model can recognize patterns very well without understanding an image in the same way a human does.
Why should we care?

Because a model can sometimes learn a pattern that works well in its training data but does not represent the medical finding we actually care about.

This is one reason researchers test models on new images, different datasets, and different patient populations. A model should not only perform well on the examples it has already seen.

A useful way to think about it is this: a medical imaging professional brings anatomy, clinical knowledge, patient information, and experience together when interpreting an image. An AI model works from the information available in its training and input data. That difference matters when we decide how AI should be used in healthcare.
My Thought

This is one of the things I find most interesting about medical imaging AI. A correct prediction does not always tell us what the model has learned.

If AI finds the right answer, does it really matter how it got there?
03 · AI & IMAGING

Can AI be confident and still be wrong?

A high percentage can look convincing. But what does it really mean?

↓
Imagine this

An AI system looks at a medical image and says:

99%
“Malignant — 99% confidence”

Sounds convincing, right?

But here is something important: confidence is not the same as correctness.

An AI model can be very confident about a prediction and still make the wrong prediction.

Why can this happen?

AI models learn from data. If a new image is very different from the examples the model learned from, the model may struggle to handle it correctly.

It may also pick up patterns in the training data that are useful for making predictions but are not actually related to the medical finding we want it to identify.

And because the model still has to produce an output, it can sometimes give that output with a high confidence score even when it is wrong.

99% confidence does not mean:
“There is a 99% guarantee that this diagnosis is correct.” The meaning of a model's confidence score depends on how the model was trained, tested, and calibrated.
Why does this matter in medical imaging?

Medical images can influence important clinical decisions. That means an AI system should not be trusted simply because a number beside its prediction looks impressive.

AI systems need proper testing, careful evaluation, and human oversight. Their role should be to support healthcare professionals rather than replace their judgment.

Researchers do not only look at whether an AI model is accurate. They can also study when it makes mistakes, whether its confidence matches its actual performance, and how it behaves on data that is different from its training examples. Understanding failure is just as important as celebrating accuracy.
My Thought

A high percentage can look reassuring. But if AI is going to assist healthcare professionals, I think one of the important questions is not only “How accurate is it?” but also “When is it wrong?”

Would you trust an AI system simply because it says “99% confident”?
